## Opportunities and challenges of playful mediation approaches

by Sebastian Senger and Ann-Kathrin Rahlwes

The computer games industry is booming, skepticism towards gaming is fading and many museums are using computer games for their educational and outreach work. Of course, digital games are fun and are now used by people of almost all ages. But how can we use digital games for museum learning? What needs to be considered in terms of game mechanics, accessibility, user-friendliness and object and visitor orientation? And what does the future of digital games in memory work look like?

We looked at digital museum games and discussed their potential for museum work using selected examples.

![](/uploads/design-ohne-titel-3.webp)

Are you planning to develop a digital game for your next exhibition? Then you should definitely read on and take part in our survey at the end!

## Four museums and their digital games

### Memory - Museum of Industrial Culture Nuremberg

The Museum of Industrial Heritage Nuremberg offers several digital games on its website. Three games resemble an analog [memory game](https://museen.nuernberg.de/museum-industriekultur/museum-digital/spiele): There are eight pairs of pictures each with images of exhibition objects. With a simple click of the mouse, you turn over the selected card. This way you can always reveal two cards. A number below the pictures shows the number of moves used so far. A text of around 400 characters next to the playing field names some of the exhibits that can be seen on the cards. However, there is no further information about the objects shown. The memory game seems to be intended for children due to its low difficulty and limited size. The incentive to play is to find identical pairs in just a few moves, as in classic memory. It is a game for one person.

![](/uploads/design-ohne-titel-1.webp)

### "Democracy and dictatorship" - LeMO

The Living Museum Online (LeMO) is a project of the Haus der Geschichte Foundation in Bonn (HdG), the German Historical Museum Berlin (DHM) and the German Federal Archives.

Two games are available on the LeMO website on the subject of ["**Democracy and dictatorship "**](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/themen/demokratie-und-diktatur.html), namely "How does the coexistence of democracy and dictatorship work?" and "How free am I?". The principle of both games is to assign photographs of objects on a map of divided Germany to the GDR or the FRG by means of a "drag and drop" function - i.e. by clicking and dragging. These are objects that are similar, such as the popular Sandman, which existed in different versions in the GDR and the FRG. Participants can find more information underneath the photos via the "Object info" link. After the assignment, a short solution to the puzzle appears under the heading "correct" or "incorrect". This offers participants direct input, deepens their knowledge of German history and encourages reflection.

![](/uploads/design-ohne-titel.webp)

### "Decision Game" - Jewish Museum Berlin

As part of the special exhibition "Looting and Restitution" (2008), the Jewish Museum Berlin (JMB) developed the ["**Decision Game**"](https://www.jmberlin.de/raub-und-restitution/entscheidungsspiel_DE.html), which visitors could play at the end of the exhibition tour and can still play online. At the beginning, one of the four animated cartoon figures shown is selected. There is a choice of "The Politician", "The Art Collector", "The Heiress" and "The Museum Director".

All characters are involved in the restitution process of a fictitious painting stolen by the Nazis in different ways. After selecting a character, an introductory text provides information about the character and their role in the restitution process. We see the character in her surroundings; for example, "The Heiress" is standing in her New York office.

The character's situation is briefly illustrated and leads to a decision dilemma: "How would you decide?" There are four different scenarios to choose from. You decide how the restitution should proceed. Behind each of these scenarios are short sequences that explain the consequences of this decision and how the story continues.

An important appeal of the game is that both positive and negative attitudes to restitution processes and their consequences can be played through at this point. There are a few surprising twists in the story here. A total of 16 variants can be played. In this way, the game illustrates different perspectives on restitution processes and the difficulties associated with restitution. In addition, this game structure can build up a high level of intrinsic motivation, as the players are actively involved in the course of the game.

The Jewish Museum Berlin advertises the game on its website under "[Online Showcase](https://www.jmberlin.de/online-schaukasten)" with the game prompt "What will become of the portrait of Max Pinselstrich? Make decisions about a fictitious painting!". The problem with this teaser is that it does not explain the context of the decision dilemmas and therefore probably does not provide sufficient incentive to follow the link.

### "Saxony-Bohemia-7000-Telelearning" - State Museum of Archaeology Chemnitz (smac)

The [game](http://p489284.webspaceconfig.de/) was released on the occasion of the special exhibition "Saxony Bohemia 7000" and is much more complex than the other games presented here. There are four mini-games and an animated explanatory film to choose from. All in the trashy style of outdated Windows versions and peppered with allusions to Bohemia and Saxony. For example, an animated and talking dumpling disk guides you through the games, in which wood carvings from the Ore Mountains appear again and again.

#### Fake postcards - smac

The first game is about recognizing fake postcards. With one click, you can turn the postcards over to see the text or image. By moving the postcards to a Windows recycle bin, you can sort out the postcards where the text and image do not match.

Contrary to what the text and images on the postcards suggest, all the postcards are fictional, humorous stories based on real historical events. The protagonist of all the stories is the fictional character Jára Cimrman, who is extremely popular in the Czech Republic and around whom numerous stories have been spun. Jára writes the postcards to his brother and recounts events from his life. The date on the postcard also gives a clue as to which real historical event is meant.

The postcard with the picture motif "Prague Embassy", for example, tells how Jára kicked a soccer over the fence of the embassy and when he climbed over the fence, hundreds of people followed him. This story alludes to the event of [September 30, 1989](https://www.bpb.de/kurz-knapp/hintergrund-aktuell/297704/vor-30-jahren-ausreise-aus-der-prager-botschaft/), when many people tried to get into the West German embassy in Prague. It is certainly not clear to all players what events the postcards allude to, but this does not play a role in the success of the game. However, it is a shame that the opportunity to provide input on historical events was missed here. The significance of the fictional character Jára Cimrman in the Czech Republic is also not addressed. The stories surrounding Jára Cimrman belong to a regionally specific cultural treasure, which means that the allusions are hardly or not at all comprehensible for players from other regions.

#### Berggeschrey - smac

The game called "Berggeschrey" is themed around mining in the Ore Mountains. The game mechanics are those of the "one-armed bandit", but it is designed as a mountain rather than a machine. With a click of the mouse, you can operate the lever and the wheels start spinning. Each time you do so, some of the coins shown next to the mountain disappear. If you happen to see the same symbol three times, a narrator's voice begins a short lecture and the pile of coins grows; you have won!

In at least twelve short presentations, you can learn about the history of mining in the Erzgebirge and its impact on the people and the regional economy - provided the increasing or decreasing pile of coins is sufficient for so many rounds. The stories are underpinned by visual changes to the mountain; when the Freiberg Mining Academy was founded, for example, the mountain was wearing a doctor's hat. Ultimately, it remains a game of chance and you never know whether you have heard all the available explanations or not.

#### Lukas Cranach - Jump `n' Run - smac

In smac's most entertaining game, you control an animated Lukas Cranach partridge. The partridge's head is the head of Lukas Cranach the Elder, cut out of a painting, and the dumpling disk is riding on it. The aim of the game is to jump over obstacles - such as gaps in the ground or letters of indulgence - with the automatically running partridge, which is located in the center of the screen while the landscape drawn in the background passes by, by clicking the mouse and collecting coins in the process.

This version of a so-called jump 'n' run game is accompanied by a narrator's voice, who talks about the life and work of Lukas Cranach. Meanwhile, animated figures cut out of historical Cranach paintings appear, which contextualize the narrative and serve as obstacles, throwing things in the way of the partridge that have to be jumped over.

Cardinal Albrecht von Brandenburg throws letters of indulgence and Jan Hus morbidly throws fire. If you come into contact with an obstacle instead of skipping it, you have to play the current section from the beginning and listen to the corresponding lecture again. The various thematically organized sections serve as levels. As the number of obstacles increases, so does the level of difficulty in each section.

Due to the mechanics of the game, you are forced to listen to the lectures several times, provided you fall into a crevice in the ground with a partridge or catch fire, for example. Repetitions are certainly useful for memorizing facts, but the facts play no role whatsoever for successful play, i.e. skilfully avoiding all obstacles. However, an individual level of fun is certainly provided by the demands on your dexterity.

#### Plunger shooting - smac

The fourth game provides information on the development of the language, culture and history of both regions in similarly short lectures. To start the narrator's voice, you have to shoot a slingshot at a target with a virtual slingshot. By clicking on the slingshot, holding it down and moving the mouse backwards or sideways, you can cock the slingshot and take aim; simply let go to shoot.

The targets are integrated into a stage set and are framed by figures and scenery designed in the style of wood carvings from the Ore Mountains. When you hit the target, the figures start to move and a short speech is played. The animated stage set and the figures play out scenes that visually accompany the narrative. A film sequence showing original footage from the Second World War stands out.

## How can we use digital games for museum learning?

What needs to be considered in terms of game mechanics, accessibility, user-friendliness and object and visitor orientation? And what does the future of digital games in memory work look like? This is what the following section is about.

### Game mechanics and object orientation

Are you planning to develop a game yourself? Then it is best to think about what you want to achieve with the game, what time and financial resources you have and which game mechanics are suitable. If you are focusing on collection objects, games based on object photographs are a good choice (examples: [Industrial Culture Nuremberg](https://museen.nuernberg.de/museum-industriekultur/museum-digital/spiele), [LeMO](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/themen/demokratie-und-diktatur.html)) and arouse curiosity about visiting an exhibition by providing surprising or in-depth insights into the history of the object.

Do you want to implement exhibition content digitally and playfully? Then perhaps a quiz, such as those implemented by the [Hygiene Museum](https://www.learningsnacks.de/share/120958/47d3e2a57079fe739fb12939e3da8fd%20589700e68) and [Heinrich Kleist Museum](https://www.heinrich-von-kleist.org/kleist-museum/kulturelle-bildung/kleist-quiz/fragen/), would be a good idea. One advantage of these simple games is the relatively low overall cost. Companies such as [involve.me](https://www.involve.me/quiz-maker) or [Museumaktiv](https://museumaktiv.de/) offer corresponding games that you only need to fill with images and information. Various other examples of digital museum games have been compiled by [Museumaktiv](https://museumaktiv.de/spiele/).

More action-packed digital museum games are based on controlling a character or an object (e.g. [smac](http://p489284.webspaceconfig.de/); [Deutsches Bergbaumuseum](https://entdecke.bergbaumuseum.de/spiel)). Different levels or areas within the game have to be mastered using skill. In some of these games, such as the jump'n'run version of [Kunstpalast](https://www.kunstpalast.de/kinder/nachts), the teaching of object history or other topics takes a back seat to the fun of the game.

In the case of the [smac](http://p489284.webspaceconfig.de/) (and the [Deutsches Bergbaumuseum](https://entdecke.bergbaumuseum.de/spiel)) game concept, game success and information transfer are linked: Those who fail cannot reach the next level and cannot try out new techniques or find out how the story continues. However, the reception of the content is optional and not decisive for the course of the game.

However, such jump'n'run games are complex to design, as a detailed user journey (storyboard about the movement of the game ends and the game events) must be created in advance and the programming is complex and error-prone.

![](/uploads/museen-konzepte.webp)

**Tip**

When developing a game, make sure it is simple and intuitive to use. Nobody who comes across your digital game by chance has the time or inclination to understand how the game works. The challenge of the game should lie in mastering tasks.

### Accessibility and user-friendliness

You can usually find digital games on museum websites under the "Digital" section or in the children's area. In the case of the four examples presented here, if you just want to find out some general information about the museum, for example if you are looking for the current special exhibition or opening times, you are unlikely to end up at one of the digital games.

Here too, you should ask yourself who you want to reach with a digital game and where or how the target group should be made aware of the offer. It can be assumed that only a few people will specifically search for a game on the website. The game should therefore find the visitors and not the other way around. As most websites today are accessed from smartphones or tablets, games should be designed responsively, i.e. the game adapts to the small screen and the alternative operation via a touchscreen.

### Orientation towards visitors

Overall, the impression prevails that the target group of the evaluated games is children and young people. One exception is the JMB's "Decision Game", which, as part of a special exhibition that was not specifically intended for children, is aimed at an adult audience.

If you want to create an offer for school classes, you should take a look at the relevant curricula. However, digital games are not just for children. Adults also like to play and are open to playful mediation formats in museums and should be considered when designing digital games. Ideally, a digital game should be designed and integrated into your online presence in such a way that it represents an independent online offering, i.e. remains understandable for players even independently of current exhibitions.

Think about which target group you want to reach with your online offering: Which age group does it belong to? What topics are they interested in? What is their level of knowledge? Do they go to museums regularly? Do they play digital games? You should consider the target group, game mechanics and content together.

### Learning in the museum

The games presented primarily address the learning dimensions of "knowledge & understanding" and "enjoyment, inspiration & creativity" (see [DMB, Leitfaden Bildung und Vermittlung](https://www.museumsbund.de/publikationen/bildungvermittlung/)). The factual knowledge presented in the games makes this clear. Informal learning, which takes place incidentally through the fun of the game, hardly ever takes place. This is where more complex games, in which the application of knowledge is relevant for successful play, can come in.

Fun or emotions can promote learning processes and make them more sustainable. If a visit to a museum - whether analog or virtual - triggers positive emotions, it rises in the visitors' favor. Having fun with digital games is not an end in itself.

The opportunity to change perspective and the resulting multi-perspectivity offered by the JMB's "[decision game](https://www.jmberlin.de/raub-und-restitution/entscheidungsspiel_DE.html)" can trigger learning processes at the level of "attitudes and values". The players think again about the exhibition content and their own points of view. If the game requires the players to think about or apply the exhibition content, they will consolidate it.

Digital games can also promote sensorimotor skills; hand-eye coordination and reaction speed are naturally required when virtually riding a partridge or shooting pellets.

## Gaming in museums: potential for educational and mediation work

Games thrive on social interaction, collaboration and competition. You can achieve this even with simple game mechanics with a multiplayer mode. Why not play a digital memory game with object photographs in pairs? This allows you to add a competitive and social component to the games. Leaderboards with the scores achieved so far by all players are also conceivable here.

An example of an award-winning, complex, location-based digital game with lots of social interaction can be found at [LWL-Museum Münster](https://deutscher-computerspielpreis.de/gewinner/spiel-des-friedens/). By linking to social media, such as the digital sharing of game results (e.g. [Victoria & Albert](https://www.vam.ac.uk/designawig)), you can create a little competition and offer your digital visitors content to share on digital networks such as Instagram or Facebook, thereby increasing your own online presence.

One advantage of digital communication is the ability to incorporate and combine different media and formats. For example, you can use videos in a digital game alongside images and texts. This would give you the opportunity to place objects and historical sources in the foreground and draw added value from the digital possibilities. Finally, visitors can move, change, playfully use or re-contextualize museum objects in the digital space.

Game mechanics that integrate the application of factual knowledge into the game would also be conducive to learning processes. If knowledge and its application contribute to the success of the game, you can expect greater learning success. High scores, different levels, awards or other experiences of success motivate players to try again; a learning process is created. You can also use digital games to convey historical narratives. Storytelling methods (such as the hero's journey) have hardly been used in digital museum games to date. There is also potential here for historical learning.

## Living culture of remembrance through digital games. An outlook

The Stiftung digitale Spielekultur has compiled a [list](https://www.stiftung-digitale-spielekultur.de/games-erinnerungskultur/) of games that take up themes from the culture of remembrance and have references to contemporary history. One example of a narrative and complex game is [Through Darkest of Times](https://www.stiftung-digitale-spielekultur.de/spiele-erinnerungskultur/through-the-darkest-of-times-2/). The aim of this game is to control a resistance group in the fight against the Nazis by making strategic decisions. Here, however, we are quickly moving into the realm of the commercial gaming industry, with which museums find it difficult to keep up financially.

Despite the strong competition, the Hamburg Memorials and Learning Sites Foundation, for example, is currently developing a game as an educational offer for school classes. The subject of the educational game "[The Children of Bullenhuser Damm](https://www.kz-gedenkstaette-neuengamme.de/nachrichten/news/neuer-kollege-fuer-das-projekt-serious-game-bullenhuser-damm/)" is the remembrance work of the 1970s around the [Memorial at Bullenhuser Damm](https://bullenhuser-damm.gedenkstaetten-hamburg.de/). The SS murdered 48 people there in April 1945. Among them were 20 children on whom medical experiments had previously been carried out. A [project](https://www.stiftung-digitale-spielekultur.de/project/lets-remember/) is running from 2023-2024, in which various museums and memorials are exploring game-based Nazi remembrance culture.

Reflecting on the influence of commercial digital games on the culture of remembrance and encouraging corresponding projects seems like an excellent idea and definitely necessary: While I was accompanying a Year 12 class as a team member on a visit to a memorial site in Buchenwald, I got talking to an 18-year-old pupil. He told me about the digital war game "[Call of Duty. WW II](https://www.stiftung-digitale-spielekultur.de/spiele-erinnerungskultur/call-of-duty-wwii/)" and talked about D-Day 1944 and the 'liberation' of Buchenwald concentration camp by the US army as if he had been there. I then tried to explain to him the difference between the digital game and historical sources and pointed out that the game probably tells us more about US-American culture of remembrance or storytelling in computer games than about the years 1944/45. He then went on to describe several times how this or that had taken place, based on his gaming experiences. I was able to start with the narrative of the 'liberation' of Buchenwald concentration camp: Because even after the SS had left the camp, the former inmates began to build structures before the US army had even reached the camp. Digital games can therefore have a significant influence on historical images and stimulate discussion.
