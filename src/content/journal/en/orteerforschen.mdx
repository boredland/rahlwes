**In November 2022, the Historisches Museum Frankfurt launched a new digital offer: the Frankfurt History App. This application provides information about the National Socialist era in Frankfurt am Main based on the history of selected sites. With the aid of an interactive map, users can find their bearings in the city and choose from over 1,000 sites in three categories: resistance, “people’s community ” (the so called “Volksgemeinschaft” ) and persecution. The app makes many of these places visible in the public space for the first time. The map also indicates the locations of Stolpersteine and monuments. In the future, the app will be expanded to cover other chapters in history as well, for example the 1848 Revolution. This blog article explains how to use the app and provides insights into our research on the sites of forced labour in Frankfurt am Main.**

            The Frankfurt History App is part of the digital memory platform [Frankfurt and National Socialism](https://frankfurt-und-der-ns.de/en/). On this platform, the Historisches Museum Frankfurt, the Jüdisches Museum Frankfurt and the Institut für Stadtgeschichte Frankfurt have brought together extensive knowledge about the history of the city under National Socialism and made it available online for the first time.

          
        
      
        
      

    
  

  

  
  

### Who developed the Frankfurt History App?

Under the oversight of the Historisches Museum Frankfurt, the Frankfurt History App provides an open venue for initiatives, associations, users, museums and archives that would like to make their own site-specific research available online. The project was funded by the Foundation Remembrance, Responsibility and Future (EVZ) and the German Federal Ministry of Finances. The technical implementation was carried out by berlinHistory e.V. after the example of the berlinHistory App. I was in charge of the historical research and the scholarly review of the texts. I also produced the texts on the sites of resistance, “people’s community” (the so called “Volksgemeinschaft”) and persecution. I based my work on research carried out by Jutta Zwilling on the city’s National Socialist topography and the texts she wrote on that topic for the special exhibition “Frankfurt and Nazism: A City Joins In” (2021/22) at the Historisches Museum Frankfurt, as well as texts by Benedikt and Jenny Jung. The Frankfurt History App texts are easy to read and offer compact knowledge on the history of the sites. 

### What information does the app offer?

The aim of the project is to provide up-to-date and inclusive mobile access to history that meets the requirements of a diverse urban society and enables engagement with history from multiple perspectives. You can decide whether you would like to look for specific types of sites, for example former bomb shelters, or have the app’s interactive map use spatial base data to show you what National-Socialism-related sites are near your present location. The app features historical photographs of many of the sites. References to specific sites makes historical knowledge tangible and—thanks in part to the numerous biographies researched within the framework of the project—the app enables an active encounter with the history of National Socialism. It provides compact and easy-to-read texts as well as information on the biographies of the persecution victims who once lived or performed forced labour at the sites. The Frankfurt History App also offers thematic guided walking tours, for example “Remembering National Socialist Euthanasia at the Main Cemetery Burial Site”. And finally, it provides in-depth information on the victim groups (“Jews under National Socialism”, “Sinti and Roma under National Socialism” and “Resistance under National Socialism”) as well as individual biographies.

### **What do I learn about the history of Jews in Frankfurt? **

By clicking on the menu item “to the map”, you can use the filter symbol at the upper right edge to find specific types of sites. Under “Sites of persecution”, for example, you will find site-specific information on Jewish institutions (synagogues, hospitals and schools) as well as private homes and businesses of citizens persecuted as Jews. In addition to well-known cases such as those of patron of the arts Dr. Arthur von Weinberg (Villa Buchenrode at Niederräder Landstrasse 38) and the collector Maximilian Freiherr von Goldschmidt-Rothschild (Rothschild Palais at Bockenheimer Landstrasse 10), the app also provides insights into hitherto unknown fates such as that of the Felsens (Waldschmidtstrasse 99), a Polish family who were deported to Poland in October 1938. The events of the 1938 November Pogroms are reflected in the destruction of synagogues and businesses as well as sites such as the Festhalle (festival hall), where a large number of Jewish men were detained before their deportation. The texts under this heading are based on research we carried out at the Hessisches Hauptstaatsarchiv (main archive of the State of Hesse) in Wiesbaden, the Institut für Stadtgeschichte (municipal archive) of Frankfurt am Main and in the databases of the Arolsen Archives and Yad Vashem. In my blog article “[How to research jewish ancestry from Frankfurt, Germany](https://rahlwes.eu/journal-en/one)”, I offer insights into our research process based on the example of the biography of Sally Fleisch.

            The wholesale wine business belonging to the Wiesengrund family was located at Schöne Aussicht 7 until 1939. The company had been founded back in 1822 and was now being operated in the third generation by Oskar Alexander Wiesengrund. It owned a wine press house in Mülheim on the Mosel as well as shares in other wine businesses. Oskar’s son was the philosopher Theodor Wiesengrund Adorno, who held a chair at the University of Frankfurt. Adorno was forced to take leave of absence immediately after the National Socialist accession to power. His teaching licence was revoked in September 1933. Soon afterwards, he emigrated to England. He lived in Oxford until 1937 and then went to the U.S. As he did not have a work permit in England, he was financially dependent on his parents, who were only able to support him, however, for a short time. On 9 November 1938, during the pogroms, the wine shop was raided, presumably by members of the SA (Storm Troopers) and the SS (Schutzstaffel: a major paramilitary organization of the Nazi party). They vandalized the rooms, wrecked the furniture, and destroyed the large stock of wine. An eyewitness reported the destruction of antique clocks and cabinets as well as two oil paintings with wine cellar motifs. Three barrels of wine each holding 5,000 litres of wine were also damaged, and 11,000 litres of wine were lost from them alone. The witness also reported that 600 bottles of wine and 300 bottles of liqueur and rum were smashed or drunk. While all this was going on, Oskar and his wife Maria Calvelli-Adorno were in their home in Oberrad, where they were attacked and arrested. After his release, Oskar Wiesengrund went about liquidating his company. He was compelled to sell off the remaining furnishings and wine below value. In early December 1938, the wine producers’ association forced the family to close down the business. Robbed of their livelihood, the couple tried to obtain a permit to leave the country. This was denied them because they had not yet paid the so-called Jewish property tax or the so-called Reich flight tax. The tax office claims amounted to over 110,000 Reichsmarks. To pay them, Oskar Wiesengrund had no choice but to pledge his securities and sell his home and business premises. In April 1939, the couple finally managed to emigrate to the USA by way of Cuba. They both died in New York, he in 1946, she in 1952. In the early 1960s, Theodor Wiesengrund Adorno successfully demanded compensation for the tremendous financial damages inflicted on his parents. There are several Stolpersteine in front of the family’s house at Seeheimer Strasse 19 in Oberrad. For further historic sites, see the [Frankfurt History App](https://frankfurt-und-der-ns.de/en/frankfurt-history-app/).

          
        
      
        
      

    
  

  

  
  

### **Was there forced labour in Frankfurt during National Socialism?**

We presently have approximately 600 indications of places in Frankfurt where forced labourers lived or worked. These indications are based on first-hand reports and research carried out by various institutions since the 1990s. So-called “foreign civilian workers” (“ausländische Zivilarbeiter”, also often referred to as “Fremdarbeiter”) were civilians from the countries of Western and Eastern Europe occupied by the Wehrmacht (the German army under National Socialism). They were recruited, often against their will, to serve as manpower, and put to work for private companies and government agencies, on farms and in households where they were also guarded. Even those who had originally come voluntarily were no longer permitted to leave their places of work; in other words they lived and worked under duress and usually under degrading conditions. Approximately one third of the civilian workers were women, and many minors were also compelled to perform forced labour. Some 5.7 million persons were employed as civilian workers in the German Reich in the summer of 1944 alone. [**Here**](https://www.zwangsarbeit-archiv.de/en/zwangsarbeit/zwangsarbeit/zwangsarbeit-hintergrund/index.html) you will find an overview of the forced labourers by country of origin. National Socialist racism led to distinctions between “Western” and “Eastern workers”. Persons from Eastern Europe were subjected to much worse treatment. For example, they received only ten per cent of the wages of a German worker and were not protected by any form of occupational safety precautions. [**more**](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/zwangsarbeit/geschichte/index.html.en)

### Where can I find information on forced labour of foreign civilian workers in Frankfurt am Main?

One good point of departure for researching the sites of forced labour in National Socialist Frankfurt is the website [Frankfurt 1933–1945](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/home) (only in german available). The site provides important research pointers and a summary of the state of research in 2003. Under the heading “Wirtschaft und Arbeit/ Hilfe zur Recherche” you will find the[ Findbuch erzählter Frankfurter Geschichte](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/beitraege/hilfe-zur-recherche-4/beitrag/findbuch-erzaehlter-frankfurter-geschichte-audio-beitraege-mit-zeitzeugen-und-zeitzeuginnen), which also contains interviews with former forced labourers. Apart from that, the “Hausstandsbücher” (residential records) kept by the district police stations from 1929 to 1945 constitute one of the most important sources for researching the use of forced labour. The residential records of several of the city’s districts (city centre, Sachsenhausen, Niederrad, Schwanheim, Griesheim and Höchst, etc.) have not come down to us for war-related reasons, but approximately [2,800 Hausstandsbücher](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/beitraege/zwangsarbeit/beitrag/die-hausstandsbuecher-als-quelle-fuer-die-zwangsarbeit-in-frankfurt) survived.

[This article](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/beitraege/zwangsarbeit/beitrag/tabellarische-uebersicht-der-firmen-institutionen-und-behoerden-die-zwangs-und-fremdarbeiter-beschaeftigt-haben) provides a complete overview of the sources used by the Institut für Stadtgeschichte for its research. In two tables available on its website, the archive lists the companies, institutions and government agencies that profited from forced labour. In addition to the places of work, the tables also document the sites of the forced labourers’ lodgings in most cases ([Tabelle 1](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/fileadmin/user_upload/table/Tabelle_Zwangsarbeiter.pdf) /[ Tabelle 2](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/beitraege/zwangsarbeit/beitrag/tabellarische-uebersicht-der-sonstigen-lager-fuer-fremd-und-zwangsarbeiter)). Further information is available on the website of the[ Landesgeschichtliches Informationssystem](https://www.lagis-hessen.de/de/subjects/index/sn/nstopo) (only in german available) and in the holdings of the Arolsen Archives. For my research, I also took a look at the state of research of the 1980s and 1990s and compared it with that of the Institut für Stadtgeschichte. Among the important sources used back then were the Hessian state government’s response to an enquiry by the Greens parliamentary group in 1984 and historical publications such as the *Heimatgeschichtlicher Wegweiser* by Ursula Krause-Schmitt et al. Ultimately, I included primarily sites whose direct connection to the forced labour system was corroborated by the comparison with archival sources and the results of research by institutions such as the Institut für Stadtgeschichte. Accordingly, there are many sources that have yet to be evaluated to identify and research further sites of forced labour.

### **The most important sources related to the forced labour of foreign civilian workers in Frankfurt am Main**

During World War II, the “labour deployment” (“Arbeitseinsatz”) department of the **Deutsche Arbeitsfront** (DAF; German Labour Front), whose Hesse-Nassau branch was based at Wilhelm-Leuschner-Strasse 69, kept a record of which companies made use of forced labourers and organized the supervision of the forced labour camps in Frankfurt. During the war years, some 25,000 forced labourers and prisoners of war were continually in service in companies, government agencies and private households. Altogether more than 49,000 persons had to perform forced labour in Frankfurt am Main between 1939 and 1945. The real number was far greater but is unknown because the “Hausstandsbücher” (police residential records) of many of the city’s districts were destroyed during the war. The DAF files are available at the **Hessisches Hauptstaatsarchiv Wiesbaden** (Bestand 483, Signaturen 705, 773, 902, 3259, 7328 and Bestand 480, Signatur 6522a).

In 1946, the **United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration** (UNRRA) launched a campaign to trace foreigners. The UNRRA was responsible for registering persons from the member states of the UN, providing for their wellbeing and helping them return to their home countries. The aim of the campaign was to clarify the whereabouts of millions of persons who had been deported. The local police stations in Frankfurt am Main reported the number of forced labourers that had lived and worked in their districts to the UNRRA. 

The **Arolsen Archives**’ digital database is also an important source for research on forced labour in Germany. In its archive tree, you will find the holdings as follows: 2 - Registration of Foreigners and German Persecutees by Public Institutions, Social Securities and Companies (1939 - 1947) >> 2.2 - Documents on the Registration of Foreigners and the Employment of Forced Laborers, 1939 - 1945 >> 2.2.0 - Forced Labor >> 2.2.0.1 - Correspondence and records on forced labor >> [Information about various prisons, labor camps, prisoner of war camps and other camps in the LK (administrative district) - 1. SK Frankfurt](https://collections.arolsen-archives.org/en/archive/2-2-0-1_9067800).

The documents indicate the country of origin of the respective forced labourer at the upper right. This is followed by an excerpt from a table listing the companies where they were exploited and the number of persons put to work there. In many cases there is also information about what the person’s subsequent fate.

My research shows that the forced labourers often did not have their lodgings on the premises of the companies they worked for. For my research, that meant that it was important to distinguish between forced labour operations and forced labour camps. The research in the aforementioned holdings also sheds light on the magnitude of exploitation through forced labour in Frankfurt and its environs. The **Frankfurt History App**’s interactive map makes the dimensions of the camp system in Frankfurt visible.

### **How can I research biographies of forced labourers?**

The [digital holdings on forced labour](https://collections.arolsen-archives.org/en/archive/2-2-0-1) under National Socialism in the Arolsen Archives are a key source. By way of a specific example, I will show you in the following how you can reconstruct a biography and make use of the Arolsen Archives database for that purpose. I start with the information provided by the “[Zwangsarbeiter](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/fileadmin/user_upload/table/Tabelle_Zwangsarbeiter.pdf)” table of the Institut für Stadtgeschichte Frankfurt am Main on the role of the German Reich Railway: This company was based at Frankfurt’s main station and ran 22 forced labour camps in the city. One of the camps was located in the dance hall of the Stapf restaurant at Oeserstrasse 16. The forced labourers lodged there worked for the Reich Railway’s express track-laying crew 6. Among the holdings in the Arolsen Archives database are the records of the railway company’s health insurance fund, containing more than 4,700 index cards with detailed information on the persons working in Frankfurt am Main.

In the archive tree, you will find the holdings as follows: 2 Registration of Foreigners and German Persecutees by Public Institutions, Social Securities and Companies (1939 - 1947) / 2.2 Documents on the Registration of Foreigners and the Employment of Forced Laborers, 1939 - 1945 / 2.2.2 Various Public Administrations and Companies (Documents related to individuals) / 2.2.2.1 War Time Card File (Registration cards, employees’ record books, individual correspondence) / [Member cards of the sickness insurance of the Deutsche Reichsbahn, Reichsbahnausbesserungswerk Frankfurt am Main (reference code 10007395)](https://collections.arolsen-archives.org/en/archive/2-2-2-1_10007395).

The following are examples of persons whose membership cards are on file in this section of the holdings and who worked in track-laying crew 6:

Giovanni AMENDOLA, index card reference code: 76432886

Remo D ANTONIO, index card reference code: 76432910

Matteo BALDUZZI, index card reference code: 76432969

Irma ANGIOLI, index card reference code: 76432901

By entering a name in the “people” search function, you can also find further documents on the respective person in other sections of the Arolsen Archives database. (For helpful pointers, see our blog article “Historical address books in research on biographies, families and places”.) In the case of Irma Angioli, for example, I found out that she had already been made to work in a [coal mine](https://collections.arolsen-archives.org/en/document/71255735) for several months before being assigned to the Reich Railway. To date we have no information on her subsequent fate.
