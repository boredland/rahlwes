**In November 2022, the Historisches Museum Frankfurt launched a new digital format: the Frankfurt History App provides information about the National Socialist era in Frankfurt am Main based on the history of selected locations. Visitors can use an interactive map to find their way around the city and choose from over 1,000 locations according to three themes: Places of Resistance, Places of the "Volksgemeinschaft" and Places of Persecution. Thanks to the app, many of these places are visible in public spaces for the first time. The map also shows stumbling blocks and memorials. This blog article provides information on how the app can be used and gives insights into my research into the places of forced labor in Frankfurt am Main.**

The Frankfurt History App is part of the digital memory platform [**Frankfurt und der Nationalsozialismus**](https://frankfurt-und-der-ns.de/de/), with which the Historisches Museum Frankfurt, the Jewish Museum Frankfurt and the Institut für Stadtgeschichte Frankfurt are making extensive knowledge about the city's history under National Socialism available online for the first time. Teachers and educators from the field of historical and political education can create tours for their lessons using their own account.

              

              

            
          
        

      

    

  

  

  
    
  
  

 
  
  

### Who developed the Frankfurt History App?

The Frankfurt History App is managed by the Historisches Museum Frankfurt and offers an open platform for initiatives, associations, users, museums and archives that want to make their own location-based research publicly accessible. The project was funded by the Foundation Remembrance, Responsibility and Future (EVZ) and the Federal Ministry of Finance. The app was technically implemented by berlinHistory e.V. based on the model of the berlinHistory app. I was responsible for the scientific editing and historical research and wrote the texts on places of resistance, places of the "Volksgemeinschaft" and places of persecution. The basis for this was the research and texts by Jutta Zwilling for the NS topography of the special exhibition "Frankfurt and the NS. A city joins in!" (2021/22) at the Historisches Museum Frankfurt as well as texts by Benedikt Burkard and Jenny Jung. The articles are written in an easy-to-understand style and offer compact information on the history of the locations.

  

 
  

  
    
  
  

  
  

### What information does the app provide?

The aim of the project is to create a contemporary, mobile and inclusive approach that meets the requirements of a diverse urban society and enables a multi-perspective approach. You can choose whether you want to visit specific places, such as former bunkers, or use the interactive map and geo-based data to see which National Socialist sites are in your vicinity. The app also provides historical photographs for many of the locations. In this way, historical knowledge becomes concrete thanks to the local reference and, thanks to the numerous biographies that were researched as part of the project, enables an active engagement with the history of National Socialism. The app offers compact and easy-to-read texts as well as information on the biographies of the persecuted people who once lived in the places or were forced to perform forced labor. As a second central feature, the app offers city tours, for example on "Remembering Nazi euthanasia at the main cemetery burial ground". Thirdly, the app provides in-depth information on the persecuted groups ("Jews under National Socialism", "Sinti and Roma under National Socialism" and "Resistance under National Socialism") and individual biographies. In the future, the range will be expanded to include other topics such as the sites of the 1848 revolution.

### What do I learn about the history of Frankfurt's Jews?

In the app, you can filter for different locations under the menu item "To map" using the filter icon at the top right.  You will also find the "Magnifying glass" icon at the top right next to the filter menu. This opens a text field that you can use to search for street names, personal and family names as well as individual projects. For example, the project "5 places - 5 stories", which was developed by a Frankfurt school group.

The "Places of persecution" section includes Jewish institutions (synagogues, hospitals and schools) as well as private homes and businesses of citizens who were persecuted as Jews. These include well-known cases such as the patron Dr. Arthur von Weinberg (Villa Buchenrode in Niederräder Landstraße 38) or the collector Maximilian Freiherr von Goldschmidt-Rothschild (Rothschild-Palais in Bockenheimer Landstraße 10), but also previously unknown fates such as that of the Polish Felsen family (Waldschmidtstraße 99), who were deported to Poland in October 1938. The events of the November pogroms of 1938 are addressed using the example of the destruction of synagogues and stores as well as places such as the Festhalle, where numerous Jewish men were detained before their deportation.

The texts in this section are based on research I conducted at the Hessian Main State Archives in Wiesbaden, the Institute for City History Frankfurt am Main and the databases of the Arolsen Archives and the Yad Vashem Museum. I explain an exemplary research method using the biography of Sally Fleisch as an example in my blog article "[How to research persecuted Frankfurt families](https://rahlwes.eu/journal/familienerforschen)".

The Wiesengrund family's wine wholesale business was located at Schönen Aussicht 7 until 1939. It had already been founded in 1822 and was run by the third generation of the family, Oskar Alexander Wiesengrund. The company owned its own wine press in Mülheim (Mosel) and also held shares in other wine merchants. Oskar's son was the philosopher Theodor Wiesengrund Adorno, who held a chair at the University of Frankfurt. Immediately after the National Socialists came to power, he was forced to take a leave of absence. In September 1933, his teaching license was revoked. Soon afterwards, he emigrated to England. He lived in Oxford until 1937 and then went to the USA. As he did not have a work permit in England, he had to rely on his parents for financial support, which they were only able to provide for a short time. During the pogroms on November 9, 1938, members of the Sturmabteilung (SA) and the Schutzstaffel (SS) of the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) probably rioted in the wine shop. They ransacked the premises, demolished the furnishings and destroyed the large wine store. A witness reported that antique clocks and cupboards as well as two oil paintings with wine cellar motifs were destroyed. Three wine barrels, each containing 5,000 liters of wine, were also damaged, causing 11,000 liters of wine to leak out. The witness also reported that 600 bottles of wine and 300 bottles of liqueur and rum had been smashed or drunk. Oskar and his wife Maria Calvelli-Adorno were in their house in Oberrad at the time. They were attacked in their home and arrested. After his release, Oskar Wiesengrund began to wind up his company. He was forced to sell the remaining furniture and wine below value. The wine industry association forced the family to close the business at the beginning of December 1938. Deprived of their livelihood, the couple tried to obtain an exit permit. This was refused, as they first had to pay the so-called Jewish property tax and the so-called Reich flight tax. The tax office's demand amounted to just over 110,000 Reichsmarks. In order to be able to pay the compulsory levies, Oskar Wiesengrund had to pledge his securities and sell his home and business premises. In April 1939, the couple were finally able to emigrate to the USA via Cuba. He died in 1946, she in New York in 1952. In the early 1960s, Theodor Wiesengrund Adorno successfully claimed compensation for the enormous financial damage inflicted on his parents.  There are several stumbling stones at the family home at Seeheimer Straße 19 in Oberrad. You can find more locations in the [**Frankfurt History App**](https://frankfurt-und-der-ns.de/de/frankfurt-history-app/)**.**

### What do I learn about forced labor during National Socialism in Frankfurt?

There are currently around 600 references to places in the Frankfurt city area where forced laborers were quartered or employed.   Currently, only the places that were researched as part of the project are visible in the Frankfurt History App. In my article [Was there forced labor during National Socialism in Frankfurt](https://rahlwes.eu/journal/gab-es-zwangsarbeit-whrend-des-nationalsozialismus-in-frankfurt), I explain which sources I used and explain how you can research forced labor in Frankfurt yourself. Since the forced laborers were often not housed in the factories where they were deployed, it was important to distinguish between forced labor factories and forced labor camps in the app. The interactive map of the **Frankfurt History App** makes the extent of the camp system in Frankfurt visible.
