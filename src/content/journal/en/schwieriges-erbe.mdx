## How realistic is the series? And what does the Disney+ mini-series have to do with Frankfurt's city history?

**The film adaptation of Annette Hess' bestseller takes us back to Frankfurt in the 1960s - right in the middle of the first Auschwitz trial, which took place from December 1963 to August 1965. The official name was '**[**Criminal proceedings against Robert Mulka and others**](https://arcinsys.hessen.de/arcinsys/showItemsList.action?nodeId=s57386)**'* (4 Ks 2/63). The Auschwitz trials are among the most important criminal trials of the post-war period. But how realistically is the first Auschwitz trial portrayed in the series "Deutsches Haus"? Does it paint a realistic picture of the social mood in West Germany? Find out where the series "Deutsches Haus" stays true to the truth - and where it dramatizes and which historical locations you can still visit today. **

### **Film set versus historical locations: Where can traces of the Auschwitz trials be found in Frankfurt?

Anyone looking for familiar Frankfurt streets in the Disney+ series will be disappointed, as the series was filmed in Poland. But the real locations are easy to find in Frankfurt:

#### **The court prison on Hammelsgasse**

The defendant [Wilhelm Boger ](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Boger)was held in custody here during the first Auschwitz trial. Although the building was demolished in 1973, a memorial plaque commemorates the fates of those who were held there under National Socialism - often for weeks without trial. Among them were victims of Nazi persecution, such as people who were involved in the resistance, or forced laborers who had resisted the inhumane working and living conditions.

In the immediate vicinity is [the former Klapperfeld police prison](https://klapperfeld.de/), which houses an exhibition about the prison under National Socialism, among other things.

#### **The Frankfurt Römer**

As there was no courtroom in Frankfurt large enough for this gigantic trial at the time, the venue was moved to the city council chamber. After all, 22 men sat in the dock. There were a total of 87 participants in the trial alone - in addition to the defendants, these included the lawyers for the defense and the joint plaintiffs as well as the public prosecutors and jurors. Large overview maps of the Auschwitz concentration and extermination camp, the Auschwitz-Birkenau camp, the crematorium and the region around Auschwitz were displayed in the courtroom. On December 20, 1963, the Auschwitz trial was opened in the City Council Chamber of Frankfurt City Hall. The Christmas market took place in front of the door. You can find more information about this [here](https://www.frankfurt1933-1945.de/beitraege/erster-auschwitz-prozess/beitrag/die-eroeffnung-des-auschwitz-prozesses-im-frankfurter-roemer) in the article by the Institute for City History.

#### **The SAALBAU Gallus**

In April 1964, the temporary courtroom in the Römer was abandoned. The trial now took place in the newly built community center in the Gallusviertel until the verdict was announced in August 1965. You can find more information about the trial venue Haus Gallus [here](https://auschwitz-prozess-frankfurt.de/index.php/prozessort-haus-gallus).

### **Post-war society and coming to terms with Nazi crimes**

The ambivalence between the spirit of optimism of the 1960s and the conservative society of the post-war period is already clear in the first scene of the mini-series "Deutsches Haus": the fictional main character Eva Bruhns, played by [Katharina Stark](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharina_Stark), is happily waiting for her fiancé. The snow-covered, gray facade of her parents' inn can be seen in the background: The Deutsche Haus on Berger Straße, which is known in Frankfurt for its pub culture. She helps out there when she is not working as a translator. Eva and her fiancé are excited because they want to make their engagement official.

But suddenly her life takes an unforeseen turn: The phone rings; a translator is urgently needed. Eva actually translates commercial contracts from Polish into German. But now she has to translate the testimony of a Polish witness - in the series he has the surname Dylka - for the Frankfurt public prosecutor's office. But Eva doesn't know the necessary vocabulary and clearly has problems with the translation. When the witness describes how Soviet prisoners of war were killed in Auschwitz, she initially translates it as if it had been a religious ceremony.

The prosecutors are irritated and ask her to look up the terms. Only now - with every word she looks up - does she realize the implications of the witness statement (season 1, episode 1, min. 11:20 - min. 15:08). This key scene makes it clear that she was not aware of Auschwitz. Her naivety is underlined by the following scene, in which she asks David Miller, an employee of the public prosecutor's office: "What was that incident Mr. Dylka told you about? Did it happen during the war?" He reacts indignantly and angrily: "You're all so ignorant."

In the series, the naive Eva is representative of a young generation of Germans who had never heard of the Auschwitz extermination camp in the early 1960s or had repressed it. After all, the crimes committed against Jews in Eastern Europe by German units had become known through the [Ulm Einsatzgruppen Trial](https://www.bpb.de/kurz-knapp/hintergrund-aktuell/547752/28-april-1958-beginn-ulmer-einsatzgruppen-prozess/) in 1958. Although the trial did not receive the same public attention as the Auschwitz trials.

  

  

  
> "Eva's naive perspective is basically modeled on that of my mother. In the early 60s, my mother was also only concerned with finding a man and getting married. ... My mother had no idea about the Holocaust and Auschwitz and didn't even see the trial. ... I think she blocked it out because she didn't want to know what her beloved father might have been involved in. ... Eva's journey is also a coming of age story, because she grows up, in a bitter way, through this realization. The name Eva is deliberately chosen."

&mdash; Annette Hess, author of the book "Deutsches Haus" and creative producer of the series of the same name, in conversation with Henning Obens, Head of Political Communication at the Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung. [1]

  

  

In general, the crimes of the National Socialist regime and the Holocaust were largely kept quiet. The Attorney General at the time [Fritz Bauer](https://fritz-bauer-der-staatsanwalt.de/) was determined to bring to justice those who had tortured and murdered people. After the founding of the Federal Republic of Germany in 1949, many perpetrators had managed to return to their professions and take up high positions. Despite the crimes they had committed during the National Socialist era, they led an unmolested life. The then Attorney General Fritz Bauer is said to have said that he was entering enemy territory when he left his office.

In January 1959, the Frankfurt public prosecutor's office began investigating 1,200 people who had worked at the Auschwitz extermination camp and questioned more than 1,000 witnesses. In the end, 22 people were charged with murder, accessory to murder and war crimes in the first Auschwitz trial. 181 survivors of Auschwitz testified.

  

  

  
> "But you have to realize that these trials are not about revenge and retribution. For us, the decisive idea here is to make the past transparent in the trial and to make a contribution to German history. For me, this is the deeper meaning of all these trials."

&mdash; Fritz Bauer (1903-1968)

  

  

Over 100 journalists from the German-speaking and international press covered the trial and reported on it in detail. As a result, the general public learned how cruel the members of the SS and the guards at Auschwitz had been. However, despite the great attention the trial received, there were also many who were not interested in it. This social ambivalence is addressed in the series "The German House". The general reluctance to come to terms with the Nazi past is shown in a scene in the kitchen of the 'German House': while the opening of the trial in the Römer is reported on the radio, a power struggle develops between Eva and her father - he switches off the radio, she switches it on again and replies, "I'd like to hear that." He switches it off with the words: "We have work to do here". Only the cook says what it's actually about: "Oh, why do you have to bring it all up again?" Eva finally hurries out of the house and into the city center to watch the indictment being read out in Frankfurt City Hall - but without telling her father. [Min. 37:44 - Min. 38:30]

  

  

  
> "When they [my fellow students] asked me over a beer in the pub in the evening what I was up to and I said that I was following the Auschwitz trial, the reaction was usually: Ah, okay. Nothing more. The interest was limited."

&mdash; Peter Kalb [4]

### **Did the translator Eva Bruhns really exist? **

#### The translators in the Frankfurt Auschwitz trials.

16 interpreters were used in the main trial; five of them were women: Erika Diedrich (Dutch); Hilde Fietel (French); Elisabeth Grimmeisen (Polish); Regina Schmidt-Ott (English) and Wera Kapkajew (Polish/Russian). While little is known about the other interpreters, Wera Kapkajew's work was followed with great interest by trial observers and journalists. She was a self-confident lawyer who - unlike Eva - was in no way overwhelmed by the task of translating the witness interviews. In addition - and Wera Kapkajew emphasized this in an interview with Bayerischer Rundfunk in 1965 - she had to submit numerous documents to the court to prove her qualifications. She and the other translators must therefore have understood the content of the witness statements - from the very beginning - and translated them adequately.

#### **Vera Kapkajew: Is she the real role model for Eva?

Wera Kapkajew grew up in Poland and studied law there. After the end of the Second World War, she worked as a translator for legal texts. She came to Germany to do her doctorate and began working as a translator at the court in Frankfurt. From 1959, she helped prepare the Auschwitz trials. During the trials, she stood out above all for her talent for translating the survivors' statements not only word for word, but also for conveying the witnesses' feelings of anger, grief and pain. Unlike in the series, [she translated the statements simultaneously](https://www.auschwitz-prozess.de/zeugenaussagen/Palarczyk-Anna/).

In 1965, she also spoke to Bayerischer Rundfunk about how she dealt with the emotionally stressful statements and how this translation work affected her private life. Wera Kapkajew was a working mother and described in the interview how difficult it was for her to watch her own child play or celebrate Christmas with him after the horrific reports of the crimes against children.

  

  

  
  
    
    
      
        
        
        
        
          
        
        
        
          
            
              

            
          
        
      
    
  
  
    

  

  

In the series, Eva is appointed as a translator in the Auschwitz trial despite her initial difficulties. The trial confronts her - and her family - with the Nazi crimes, her own family history and the actions of her parents during the war. Even the first translation of a contemporary witness report leaves a deep impression on Eva, as another scene makes clear: she stands in front of the "Deutsches Haus" restaurant and looks through a window at the people in the dining room. As she enters, her mother speaks to her: "What happened to you? You're as white as the wall." However, there is no conversation - which must have been typical of family circumstances at the time.

A short time later, Eva sees one of the Polish survivors, Dr. Rachel Cohn, at the Frankfurt Christmas market. She has traveled to the trial and turns to passers-by for help because she can't find her way to the boarding house, but mainly experiences rejection and rejection. Eva accompanies her to the boarding house - to the displeasure of her sister, who tries to escape the situation (season 1, episode 1, min. 29:02- min. 30:48). But is this a realistic picture of the situation of the witnesses who traveled from all over the world for the trial?

### **The witnesses in the Auschwitz trial**

The Frankfurt public prosecutor's office began investigating the crimes at Auschwitz in the spring of 1959. At the time, Fritz Bauer had received documents proving that members of the SS had deliberately killed prisoners in Auschwitz. He applied to the Federal Court of Justice in Karlsruhe for the Frankfurt Regional Court to be responsible for the legal investigation of all crimes committed in the Auschwitz concentration camp. He put together a group of public prosecutors who had not themselves taken part in the Nazi crimes and were committed to solving them. They collected evidence and witness statements to prepare the indictment of former members of the SS. They were supported by [Hermann Langbein](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Langbein), the co-founder of the International Auschwitz Committee. [2]

The [Frankfurt fashion entrepreneur and historian Arno Lustiger](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/XAVRH0uPCH6uKA) was also questioned as a [witness to the crimes in the Auschwitz-Blechhammer concentration camp by the Frankfurt public prosecutor's office](https://arcinsys.hessen.de/arcinsys/showArchivalDescriptionDetails?archivalDescriptionId=824971) and testified against several members of the SS. He followed the proceedings himself as a listener.

  

  

  
> "He (Herman Leinkenjost) was an SS-Unterführer and was known in Blechhammer as 'Tom Mix' because he constantly walked around the camp with two pistols in his hand. In the camp itself I only saw that he often mistreated prisoners. ... On the evacuation march to Gross-Rosen, I saw for myself how he murdered 10 to 15 prisoners with pistol shots."

&mdash; Arno Lustiger, January 23, 1961

  

  

  
  

#### **The situation of the witnesses in Frankfurt**

The trial against 23 members of the SS and one prisoner functionary finally began a few days before Christmas 1963. This is also the time frame in which the series is set. Many of the witnesses traveled from all over the world - most of them did not speak German, often had no money and were on their own after their arrival in Frankfurt. The journey was psychologically stressful for them, especially as many were scarred by years of imprisonment in concentration camps. She also had to expect to meet some of the defendants on the way to court, as not all of them were in custody. Some were even accommodated in the same hotel as the defendants and met them at breakfast. [5]

  

  

  
> "German was ... the language of the enemy for many foreigners and when he heard German, the first reaction was; here is a representative of those who did this and that to me. That may not sound nice today. It was a reality. And now a witness like that comes along and doesn't know where to sleep. And doesn't know where the court is. And can't take a cab because he can't pay for it. So that was a situation that was intolerable."

&mdash; Hermann Langbein, 1993 [5]

  

  

It was only a newspaper article that drew attention to the difficult situation of the witnesses and triggered a privately organized aid initiative in April 1964, which was launched by the Frankfurt women Emmi Bonhoeffer and Ursula Wirth. The group picked up the witnesses at the airport or train station, accompanied them to the hotel and stood by them in court. In the first year alone, they accompanied 170 survivors to court. [3]

  

  

  
> "I want to go with the witness Stachek Kamiński to the public gallery on the second floor. We get into the elevator. The door is already closing when suddenly a hand holds the door open. A lady and a younger woman get in. I notice something happen to Stachek immediately. He turns pale and starts to tremble. The elevator goes up, we get out, the women walk away. I ask: 'Stachek, what's going on?' He asks: 'Do you know who that was? Of course I didn't know. 'That was Hedwig Höß and her daughter, the wife of Rudolf Höß, the camp commandant of Auschwitz.

&mdash; Peter Kalb, then witness counselor and student at the Institute for Social Research, 2024 [4]

  

  

In the series, the translator Eva sits next to the wife of Robert Mulka on the first day of the trial. As SS Hauptsturmführer and adjutant to Rudolf Höss, he was responsible for ordering Zyklon B and was one of the main defendants. "Do we know each other?" asks Eva. "Not that I know of," replies Mrs. Mulka. Eva ponders further: "Maybe from the "German House..."? "We're from Hamburg," Mrs. Mulka brusquely dismisses her (Season 1, Episode 1, Min.: 39:55 - Min.40:56). Then the indictment is read out.

### **Fact check: How realistic is the representation of the process?

#### The reading of the indictment (episode 1)

The depiction of the trial itself is quite extensive in the series. The witness statements, the reading of the indictment, but also the perpetration of the camp are given an extraordinary amount of space. The author [Annette Hess](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annette_Hess) had worked intensively with the tape recordings of the trial, which have been available on the Fritz Bauer Institute website for several years, and incorporated them into the series.

  

  

  
> "... the reading of the indictment ... takes about seven minutes in the series. In reality, it was 800 pages and the reading took two days. I worked on the montage for a long time. I wanted people to understand the sheer volume of crimes on the one hand, but also to grasp them emotionally on the other. Numbers remain abstract, but individual fates are touching. When you mention a name, it becomes a person, so: 'The prisoner Rudeck died after doing sports. And then the assessor keeps repeating: 'in an indeterminate number of cases'. He sometimes speaks incorrect German, the sentences are not completed. It's an artificial text. But for me, the scene reflects exactly what I wanted to achieve. Also thanks to the great actor Mathias Luckey and our way of resolving the film."

&mdash; Annette Hess [1]

  

  

  
  

#### **Rachel Cohen testimony (episode 2)**

The testimony of Auschwitz survivor Rachel Cohen, played by Iris Berben, is particularly impressive. She testifies in the trial against the SS pharmacist Josef Hartlieb, who is probably modeled on the defendant Victor Capesius. She describes how her twins were selected for gassing at the so-called "ramp" - and identifies the SS pharmacist (season 1, episode 2, min. 37: 53 - min. 40:35). After her testimony, she makes an angry plea against the defendants, who allegedly did not know what had happened in Auschwitz. She even approaches their benches, threatens them with her finger and shouts at them (season 1, episode 2, min. 43:45 - min. 44:57). Rachel Cohn then leaves the court, is hit by a car and dies.

The character of Rachel Cohen is fictitious, but appears to be based on the testimony of the Hungarian doctor and survivor [Mauritius Berner ](https://www.auschwitz-prozess.de/zeugenaussagen/Berner-Mauritius/), who testified in court on August 17, 1964. He had been deported to Auschwitz in July 1944 with his wife and three daughters, who were murdered immediately upon arrival. He testified against [Victor Capesius ](https://www.auschwitz-prozess.de/materialien/M_03_Angeklagte-Biographien), whom he and other prisoners knew from the time before the war. At that time, Capesius had worked as a representative for I.G. Farbenindustrie and had had business and private dealings with many doctors and their families. They met him again in the Auschwitz concentration camp; now he was on the side of the National Socialists, selecting people and sending many to be murdered in the gas chambers - including the family of Mauritius Berner.[12] He also worked alongside the doctor Josef Mengele, who carried out medical experiments on identical twins.

Rachel Cohn's plea in the series is based on the testimony of survivor and witness Josef Glück. Unlike in the series, he did not shout, stand up or seek a confrontation. His voice sounds angry, desperate, sad, accusing. The entire recording can be listened to [here](https://www.auschwitz-prozess.de/zeugenaussagen/Glueck-Josef/).

  

  

  
> "Now I have to say something else, please. I have read in the newspapers here that the gentlemen here say that they did not know what was actually going on in Auschwitz. I must tell you that I already knew everything on the second day I was there. But not only me. This little boy, please, he was 16 years old. His name is Andreas Rapaport. He was in the 11th barrack. He shouted to me: 'Uncle, I know I have to die. Tell my mother that I thought of her until the last moment. But I couldn't tell her, her mother died too. Please, this little boy, he knew what was there. And the gentlemen didn't."

&mdash; Josef Glück [14]

  

  

  
  

#### **The strategy of defense (episode 2)**

The testimony of another witness, Jan Habuda, makes it clear that the defense wanted to cynically convict the witnesses of giving false testimony (season 1, episode 2, min. 11: 38 - min. 14:23). That certainly happened in the real trial. The defense repeatedly tried to unsettle the witnesses with questions about an exact date or time and publicly question their credibility when they reported their experiences. In the series, Habuda is put through the wringer by the defense because he testifies that he saw the accused Josef Hartlieb on the ramp during the selection on a certain day, but the defense presents evidence to the contrary. Hermann Langbein later summed up how absurd these manoeuvres by the defense were, considering that the prisoners had neither calendars nor watches.

#### **Wilhelm Boger and the "Boger Swing" (episode 4)**

In the fourth episode, another character is introduced: [SS-Oberscharführer Wilhelm Boger,](https://www.auschwitz-prozess.de/zeugenaussagen/Gestaendnis_des_Angeklagten_Boger/) the cruel head of the political department in Auschwitz. It was a survivor's complaint against Wilhelm Boger in the spring of 1958 that got the investigation rolling in the first place. Boger tortured his victims with the so-called "Boger swing", on which the victims were tied upside down by their arms and legs and abused beyond recognition. [The model used in the trial can be seen in the Historical Museum.](https://historisches-museum-frankfurt.de/de/node/33966) However, the replica shown in the series is much larger.  Former prisoners' wives who had worked for Boger as secretaries and had witnessed the interrogations in the next room also testified in court. In the series, the defense tried to imply that they and the other witnesses against Boger had not witnessed the torture and therefore could not testify to it: "Allegedly there, allegedly heard, allegedly seen. I doubt the credibility of the witnesses. ... There is not a single witness who experienced it first-hand." A viewer shouts: "Because they're dead." (Season 1, episode 4, min. 20:38 - min. 20:50). In fact, the witness Erna Krafft testified against Boger. She had been tortured by him with a whip. [Her testimony was recorded on tape and can be accessed via the website of the Fritz Bauer Institute](https://www.auschwitz-prozess.de/zeugenaussagen/Krafft-Erna/).

The series aptly illustrates in the Boger case, but also in other cases, that the perpetrators showed no remorse or awareness of wrongdoing. What is not discussed, however, is that Fritz Bauer saw the Auschwitz concentration camp as a "murder machine", as is common in today's jurisprudence, and considered any involvement in it sufficient to convict members of the SS. [7]

#### **The site visit to Auschwitz (episode 4)**

The fourth episode of the series also includes a visit to Auschwitz (season 1, episode 4, min. 37:40 - min. 47:13). Here, Eva remembers her childhood, when she lived near the camp with her parents and sister. Over the course of the series, this was hinted at again and again, but Eva couldn't remember it at first. But then she finds out that her father worked as a cook in the camp's casino.

The site visit to Auschwitz, which actually took place, is relevant in several respects: Firstly, the camp and its dimensions are made tangible for the judges, prosecutors and other trial participants themselves. Secondly, it examines whether the witnesses' statements - for example about shootings at the so-called "Black Wall" - were accurate. According to the legal situation at the time, murder had to be proven on an individual basis, so the examination of the visual conditions in Auschwitz was crucial for the trial and the verdict. Looking back, prosecutor Gerhard Wiese also remembers that the famous statement by the main defendant Robert Mulka that he knew nothing about the gassings could not be true:

  

  

  
> "After the gate, 'Arbeit macht frei', you come directly to the first barracks buildings and they are so relatively close together that, for example, Mr. Mulka, the adjutant, who claimed of himself, I don't know that [meaning: the gassings], I wasn't told that, I don't know that, I can't ... Well, he practically saw everything on his doorstep, so, such foolish behavior."

&mdash; Gerhard Wiese [13]

  

  

The account in the series, according to which documents were found and handed over in Auschwitz during the visit that prove Mulka's complicity beyond doubt, also does not correspond to the historical facts. As early as 1959, the Frankfurt journalist Thomas Gnielka had received corresponding original documents from an Auschwitz survivor. [8] They were therefore already available at the beginning of the trial.  This raises the question as to why the real historical events were deviated from in this way. The scenic representation also deviates considerably from the real events: While only a small group makes the site visit in the series, the actual group - consisting of a representative of the Frankfurt court, four public prosecutors, three joint plaintiffs' representatives, 13 defense lawyers and one defendant - was accompanied by numerous international journalists in reality.

  

  

  
  
    
    
      
        
        
        
        
          
        
        
        
      
    
  
  
    

  

  

  
  

#### **The testimony of Eva Bruhn's mother (episode 5)**

The examination of the family's involvement in the Nazi regime's policy of extermination reaches its climax in the fifth episode. This is where Eva's mother, played by Anke Engelke, is questioned. She admits that she was in Auschwitz with her family and knew about the gassings. The mother's statement explains an earlier clash with Mulka, which was seen in episode two. According to the series' narrative, Eva's parents had reported Mulka to the Reich Security Main Office because he had made disparaging remarks about Goebbels and doubted the victory of the German military. Such a denunciation had indeed taken place.

After the court hearing, Eva breaks up with her parents and moves into a hotel.

  

  

  
> "Then there are ... fictional secondary characters, but they are all based on prototypes of the time. For example, there is a young public prosecutor, David Miller, who is Jewish and who suffers immensely from the fact that his family was spared from the Holocaust. I didn't realize for many years that the survivors also had to bear the burden of having survived. That's why this character was so important to me, because I really wanted to tell this aspect."

&mdash; Annette Hess [11]

  

  

  
  

### **More figures in the series**

  

  
  

#### **Who is David Miller?

There is one character in the series who is difficult to categorize: David Miller, a young public prosecutor, comes from Canada, speaks fluent German and is always conspicuous for his quick temper. He is Jewish and suffers from survivor's guilt. In the series, he appears as a member of the Frankfurt public prosecutors investigating the Auschwitz trial. In fact, the character is completely fictional. The Frankfurt public prosecutors in the trial consisted exclusively of German lawyers who had not participated in the crimes committed under National Socialism: Hanns Großmann was the senior public prosecutor of the public prosecutor's office at Frankfurt am Main Regional Court and led the criminal proceedings against Mulka and the other members of the SS. Georg Friedrich Vogel, Joachim Kügler and Gerhard Wiese were also involved. [9] There are only a few parallels to the joint plaintiff's representative Henry Ormond, actually Hans Oettinger. In 1939, he fled Germany, where he had been persecuted as a Jew and briefly imprisoned in the Dachau concentration camp. He initially came to Canada via detours. In 1943, he changed his name to Henry Lewis Ormond and joined the British army. In 1950, he opened a law firm in Frankfurt and represented numerous victims of National Socialism in court. [10]

#### **Who is Fritz Bauer?

Fritz Bauer himself only plays a minor role in the series, which becomes clear in the very first episode when he stands on the sidelines of an interrogation and does not intervene. This is probably also due to the fact that Bauer and his significance for the Auschwitz trials have been the subject of several films in recent years. Films such as "Im Labyrinth des Schweigens" (2014), "Der Staat gegen Fritz Bauer" (2015) and "Die Akte General" (2016) have examined Bauer as a person in detail and captured the encrusted and authoritarian social conditions of the 1960s in a similar way to the series "Deutsches Haus". In retrospect, Fritz Bauer is of central importance for the legal reappraisal of National Socialist crimes. This is because today's jurisprudence corresponds fairly closely to Bauer's view that any involvement in the industrial extermination at Auschwitz - and not just a specific act - should be sufficient for a conviction.

  

 
  
  

## **Is the "Deutsches Haus" series suitable for lower and upper secondary schools? Didactic impulses for the classroom**

The series provides an easy introduction to the topic of coming to terms with the crimes of National Socialism and also deals with topics such as repression and guilt. However, the characters in the series and some of the scenes do indeed bring together complex themes and narrative threads. Especially as some scenes work with gestures and actions with a highly symbolic effect, which should be analyzed as part of a classroom discussion. With this in mind, I recommend the series for lessons in German and history at upper secondary level. Even though the mini-series largely works with fictional characters, the testimonies in the trial are based on historical witness statements that can be listened to on the website of the Fritz Bauer Institute. The series therefore offers an interesting framework for teaching critical work with sources.

**Learning objective:** The critical examination of the dramaturgical staging trains the media competence of learners in dealing with popular cultural images of history.

**Target group:** Secondary level II

### **Suggestions for dealing with the series characters in class**

#### **Following 1**

**Eva Bruhn: **In the series, she is representative of the unsuspecting post-war generation.

- **The task:** Pupils compare the film production of the translator Eva with the biography of the real interpreter **Vera Kapkajew**. The interview with Wera Kapkajew (linked above) serves as a basis. Were the translators back then really as naive and ill-prepared as portrayed in the series?

- **The focus:** What dramaturgical function does a "naive" main character like Eva fulfill for the series? To what extent would the narrative perspective have changed if the main character had been a seasoned lawyer like Wera Kapkajewdie? Which character can we as viewers identify with more easily?

#### **Following 2**

**Rachel Cohen:** Hardly any other witness character is given as much space in the series as Rachel Cohen. This fictional character is based on the testimonies of the two survivors Josef Glück and Mauritius Berner.

- **The task:** Students listen to the original recording of the testimony of Josef Glück and Mauritius Berner (linked above). What is the tone of voice? How does the series character Rachel Cohen appear in court in comparison?

- **The focus:** Rachel Cohen seeks confrontation with the defendants in the series: she stands up, screams, seeks eye contact with the accused. Shortly after her testimony, she dies in a car accident. Think about why this dramatic staging was chosen.
