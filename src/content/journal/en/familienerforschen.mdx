#### From the series "Biographies on the trail".

In this blog article, I explain how you can research Jewish families who lived and were persecuted in Frankfurt am Main during the National Socialist era (1933 to 1945). I go into the most important sources - from research in historical address books to the central sources in the regional and state archives. As an example, I use the biography of Sally Fleisch from Frankfurt, which I compiled for the Frankfurt History App on behalf of the Historisches Museum Frankfurt am Main. These references are therefore useful for both family research and biographical research.

## Online research in the historical Frankfurt address book

The [Frankfurt Address Books ](https://sammlungen.ub.uni-frankfurt.de/periodika/nav/classification/8688176) from 1834 to 1943 in the digital collection of the University Library of Goethe University Frankfurt am Main. Select a year, call up the name register and search for your person. You can also look up the residential and business address in the street register.

The 1933 Frankfurt address book tells us the following about Sally Fleisch:

- he was a master butcher

- lived in Rödelheim

- the house at Reichsburgstraße 2 belonged to him, because owners are identified in the address book by the letter E

- he had a telephone connection

- There were other companies and businesses in the building, such as a dye works

- he probably lived on the 2nd floor with his family

#### Which archive holdings are suitable for researching a Frankfurt biography?

### The so-called "zero card index" at the Institute for City History Frankfurt am Main

The "zero card index" documents Frankfurt citizens in the period from around 1868 to 1932. However, the inventory is very incomplete due to the war. For a fee, the team at the Institute of City History will research individuals for you. As the collection is still subject to the protection periods of the Hessian Archive Act, you cannot search it yourself.

### The household registers in the Institute for City History Frankfurt am Main

These are the residents' registration records from 1932 onwards and contain information on the residents by house, namely their date and place of birth, marital status, religion, nationality and whether they moved away from Frankfurt or died. However, the inventory is very incomplete due to the war. For a fee, the team at the Institute of City History will research people for you. As the collection is still subject to the protection periods of the Hessian Archive Act, you cannot search it yourself.

### The [Arcinsys](https://arcinsys.hessen.de/arcinsys/einfachesuche.action) database

In Arcinsys you can search the holdings of numerous Hessian archives. Even if you don't find anything straight away, you can write to the archive to find out whether there are any documents relating to the person you are looking for. This is because not all holdings are already recorded in the archive databases. Some, in turn, cannot be searched online due to retention periods. To ensure that you find the right results quickly, it is worth narrowing down your search. If you are looking for a person from Frankfurt, for example, you will find documents in the Institute for City History and/or the Hessian Main State Archive in Wiesbaden. If you have found a person in the 1933 address book, it is best to search in a period of +/- 20 years - i.e. 1913 to 1953. We assume, so to speak, that the person was of age, employed and registered in Frankfurt in this fictitious period. The archive system contains further information on people who were listed on the file covers. In this case, we learn that Sally Fleisch was born on October 8, 1878 in Frankfurt Rödelheim and that he was married to Selma Sternfels.

To order files for inspection in the archive, you must create an account with Arcinsys and complete a request for access. Among other things, you must explain why you want to view the files. In principle, however, archives are open to everyone, which means that you do not have to be a historian to view files. However, files from before 1920 contain many handwritten notes in Kurrent or Sütterlin, which you may not be able to decipher straight away. German Kurrent script is a cursive script that was commonly used in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Sütterlin script was taught in schools from the 1920s onwards. However, many archives regularly offer [training](https://www.stadtgeschichte-ffm.de/de/veranstaltungen/kalender/549/mit-kriminalakten-alte-schriften-lesen-lernen-einfuehrung-in-die-palaeographie-des-19-jahrhunderts) courses on handwriting and there are often alphabets in Kurrent or Sütterlin available. The Geheime Staatsarchiv preußischer Kulturbesitz (Prussian Secret State Archives) has digitally published some [work aids](https://gsta.preussischer-kulturbesitz.de/nutzung/arbeitshilfen/schriftbeispiele.html) on this subject.

You can find out what results the archive research in the Hessian Main State Archives in Wiesbaden and in the Institute for City History Frankfurt am Main yielded about Sally Fleisch in the [Frankfurt History App](https://frankfurt-und-der-ns.de/de/frankfurt-history-app/) at his address Reichsburgstraße 2 in Frankfurt-Rödelheim.

### The database [LAGIS](https://www.lagis-hessen.de) (Hessen State Historical Information System)

#### Find birth certificates, marriage certificates and death certificates

You can access the Hessian register of births, marriages and deaths via Lagis. The city of Frankfurt documented marriages from 1849 and births and deaths from 1851. Before that, these events were recorded by the churches. The birth register of the city of Frankfurt can be viewed online up to and including the year 1899. The records of marriages can be viewed up to 1928 and deaths up to 1958. The volumes are arranged chronologically and contain an index of names at the end of each volume. In many cases, the name index is also available in a separate volume. First you enter the name of the town in the database and can then narrow down the search further, for example by decade and by the type of register (births, marriages or deaths).

#### **On a birth certificate you will find the following information about the child's parents:**

- the surname (including the mother's maiden name)

- the profession

- the place of residence and address

- the religious affiliation

and the following information about the child:

- the date and time of birth

- the gender

- the full name

and this general information:

- the name of the registrar

- the date on which the birth certificate was issued

#### **Deciphering birth certificates in Kurrent**

Transcription of Sally Fleisch's birth certificate (last screenshot in the picture gallery):

Page 161, number 161: "Roedelheim, on October 14, 1878 ... the butcher Leopold Fleisch residing at Roedelheim in Wehrstraße No. 6 of the Israelite religion ... reported that a child of the male sex was born to Jette Fleisch, née Stern, his wife of the Israelite religion, living with him in Roedelheim in his home on the eighth of October of the year one thousand eight hundred and seventy and eight in the afternoon at twelve and a half o'clock, who was given the first name Sally ... read aloud, approved and signed by Leopold Fleisch ... The registrar Müller ..."

You can practise reading the Kurrent script by comparing this transcription with the screenshot of the birth certificate.

### Search for birth, marriage and death certificates at the Frankfurt registry office

The registry offices in Mitte and Höchst are handing over records of births 110 years ago, marriages 80 years ago and deaths 30 years ago to the state archives. This year (2023), the birth register for 1913, the marriage register for 1943 and the death register for 1993 will be handed over.

For your research this means: If you are looking for birth certificates from 1913 onwards, the [Frankfurt registry office](https://frankfurt.de/service-und-rathaus/verwaltung/aemter-und-institutionen/standesamt) is the right address for you. There is a fee for the search. Please send your request by post to the following address:

Registry office Frankfurt am Main

Bethmannstr. 3

60311 Frankfurt am Main

Information can only be obtained by those who are either related to the person or have been commissioned by a relative to carry out the search.

### Research in the holdings of the International Tracing Service Bad Arolsen, today [Arolsen Archives](https://arolsen-archives.org)

The archive preserves documents on the victims and survivors of National Socialism. Information on around 17.5 million people can be found in the collection. There are two ways to search the online database: 1. by name, 2. by context of origin of the files (archive tree)

The archive files are continuously indexed, so that many documents are already indexed. Documents can also be searched for in the archive tree. However, these are not sorted by name, which makes searching for them much more time-consuming.

#### **Search by name:**

You can enter the name in the search bar on the left-hand side (third image in the gallery) - the selected document will then appear on the right-hand side. In the top line, which is highlighted here in the third image, you can see in which collection this document is located. As this search involves documents that are individually indexed and linked, you must click on the last menu item in the top line in order to see other documents from the collection. The documents from this collection on the deportations from Frankfurt are copies that the US Zone Division made of the originals after liberation. The originals from the Frankfurt/Main State Police Office (Gestapo) can be viewed in the Hessian State Archives in Darmstadt. The documents show that Sally Fleisch was deported from Frankfurt to the Lodz ghetto on October 20, 1941.

#### **Presentation of the research results on Sally Fleisch in the** [**Frankfurt History App**](https://frankfurt-und-der-ns.de/de/frankfurt-history-app/)

![](/uploads/design-ohne-titel-2.webp)

This apartment building belonged to Sally Fleisch and his wife Selma (née Sternfels), who lived and worked here with their three children. Sally Fleisch was a master butcher and had taken over the business from his father in 1926. As a result of the boycotts against Jewish businesses, sales fell noticeably from 1933 onwards. The butcher's shop had to close in June 1938. The couple had to pay 12,500 Reichsmark (RM) as a "Jewish property levy". The three children managed to emigrate abroad: 22-year-old Herta went to South Africa with her fiancé in 1937. 19-year-old Heinrich emigrated to Northern Rhodesia via South Africa in 1938. The youngest brother Walter attended the Jewish training workshop in Fischerfeldstraße, where young people were prepared for their emigration. He was able to leave for England in 1939. In 1938/39, the family was forced to sell all the properties they owned, including fields in Rödelheim, Sossenheim and Eschersheim as well as a stable in Wehrhofstraße. Her house at Reichsburgstraße 2 was bought by her non-Jewish banker Dr. Fritz Muth, the owner of her bank J. Muth & Co, based in Rödelheim, for 70,000 RM. In February 1939, Sally Fleisch applied to the Frankfurt foreign exchange office to receive more money each month. The accounts of Jews were blocked by the foreign exchange office at this time. It determined how much the account holders were allowed to withdraw each month. Anyone who needed more money to support their family, like Sally, had to submit an application. Sally Fleisch not only supported his son so that he could complete his education, he also supported his wife's brother, who was injured in the war and needed help. On February 27, 1940, the foreign currency office decided that Sally could only have 250 RM a month at his disposal. Because the money was not enough to live on, the couple moved in with Sally Fleisch's sister, Paula Nussbaum, who lived at Kettenhofweg 94, on March 18, 1940. She also gave him securities to ensure his livelihood. On October 20, 1941, the couple were deported to Lodz and murdered. Their assets were confiscated a month later. However, the banker Dr. Fritz Muth, with whom the Fleisch family's account was held, complained about this. He had also been the buyer of the house. He now claimed that he was entitled to part of the Fleisch family's money, as they had sold him the house without drawing his attention to the considerable damage to the roof. He therefore wanted to use some of the money from their account to have the roof repaired.
