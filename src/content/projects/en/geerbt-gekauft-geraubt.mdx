From May 17 to October 14, 2018, the Historisches Museum Frankfurt am Main presented the results of its provenance research on the painting and silver collection. The exhibition used historical sources to trace the unlawful acquisition of objects by Frankfurt museums under National Socialism.

## Research. Understanding. Storytelling.

I drew up an exhibition script, selected objects, researched images and sources, requested image rights and developed generally understandable exhibition texts based on my research. On behalf of the museum, I also researched six Frankfurt families who were disenfranchised, dispossessed and persecuted under National Socialism because they were Jewish. In the post-war period, they fought to receive compensation and have their property restituted. To this end, I researched the Hessian Main State Archives in Wiesbaden, the Frankfurt Institute for City History and the Berlin State Archives. The results were presented in the exhibition in the form of biographical dossiers.

### Stadtlabor exhibition

On the occasion of the exhibition "Inherited. Bought. Looted?", the Stadtlabor of the Historisches Museum Frankfurt am Main (HMF) offered a [workshop for Frankfurt citizens](https://historisches-museum-frankfurt.de/de/geerbt_gekauft_geraubt). Participants brought along objects for which it was unclear whether they had been legally or illegally acquired during the Nazi era. In one case, the aim was to research the history of a residential building and its residents. As an expert, I supported the participants in their archive research and helped them to transcribe and analyze documents from the National Socialist era.

The workshop was led by Angela Jannelli, curator at the HMF, and pedagogue Gottfried Kößler. Johannes Beermann-Schön, archivist at the Fritz Bauer Institute, was also among the experts advising the participants.

#### Accompanied archive research

In the booklet accompanying the exhibition, I reported together with Johannes Beermann on the working process that the participants in the urban laboratory project went through. In the run-up to the first session, they were asked to consider questions such as "What history do you associate with this object?" or "What led you to assume that it was an object with a Nazi past?". The urban lab assistants presented the objects they had brought with them in the first session and explained, for example, "What makes it a 'difficult thing' for me?". Expectations and fears - especially with regard to what the research might reveal - were also discussed here. The aim of the first session was to gather information about the objects: Where does the object come from? What is known about its history? What do I know so far about my family's history under National Socialism? The last question was particularly important, as researching the origin of the object always required an examination of the history of its previous owner, i.e. their own parents, grandparents or great-grandparents. Many participants said that they were relieved to finally be able to work through the history behind their "difficult object". The work process and the participants' research results were recorded in the form of [animated films](https://historisches-museum-frankfurt.de/de/geerbt_gekauft_geraubt/videos).
